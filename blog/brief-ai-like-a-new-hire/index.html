Brief Your AI Tools the Way You Would Brief a New Hire

You would never hire a new receptionist, point at the front desk, and say "figure it out." Yet that is how most local businesses set up their AI tools: switched on, handed a website link, and left to guess. The tool is rarely the problem. The brief is. An AI tool is a new hire that never sleeps, never forgets, and does exactly what you told it — which is why telling it clearly matters more than buying the cleverest tool on the market.

Nona Digi · November 20, 2026 · 5 min read

The tool is only as clear as the brief

Think about the last person you trained properly. You did not hand them a manual and walk away. You told them what a good day looks like, which customers to watch for, what to say when someone is upset, and when to come and get you. You checked in after the first week and corrected the small things before they became habits. That is a brief, and it is the whole job of managing someone new.

AI tools need the same thing, for the same reason. They do not know your business. They do not know that your best customers are landlords with three properties, or that a caller asking about a leak might mean a burst pipe or a slow drip under a sink — two very different urgencies. Everything the tool knows about your business is what you put in the brief. Leave it vague and you get vague back, at scale, every hour of every day.

What a good brief contains

A useful brief is short enough to fit on one page and specific enough that a new hire could act on it. Five parts cover almost everything:

  • The one job. Not "help with customers" — one job, stated plainly. "Answer questions from people visiting our website and book them into an appointment." If the tool has two jobs, write two briefs.
  • The customer. Who is asking, and what are they worried about? A homeowner with no hot water at 6 in the morning is not browsing. They want to know if you can come today and what it will cost.
  • Your words. The phrases you actually use. "Same-day repair," "upfront flat-rate pricing," "licensed and insured." An assistant that speaks your words sounds like your business. One that invents its own sounds like a stranger.
  • The lines it never crosses. What it must never promise, guess at, or quote. Prices you have not set. Dates you cannot keep. Safety advice it is not qualified to give. Write the lines down — "never guess" is a policy, not a hope.
  • When to hand it to a human. The exact moment the conversation leaves the script: an angry customer, a job you have never done before, anything involving money changing hands. A good brief names the handoff, not just the job.

A worked example

Say you run a plumbing company in Orlando, and the AI Chat Receptionist on your website answers visitors in chat. Your old setup was a chat box that answered "How can I help?" and not much else. Your new brief says: "You answer questions from homeowners on our website. Your job is to find out what is wrong, say whether we can come today, and book the visit. Use our words: same-day repair, upfront flat-rate pricing. Never quote a price we have not published. If the customer mentions flooding, a burst pipe, or no water at all, treat it as urgent and offer the next available slot first. If they are upset, or the job is something we have never done, hand the conversation to the office." That is about a hundred words — and it is the difference between a tool that chats and a tool that books work.

Notice what the brief does not contain: no jargon, no settings menus, no talk of models or prompts. It reads like the notes you would leave for a new starter on their first day. That is the test. If a person could not do the job from your brief, the tool cannot either.

The ten-minute weekly check-in

A brief is not a document you write once. New hires get a first-week review, and your AI tools should too. Once a week, read a handful of real conversations — five is enough — and ask three questions:

  1. Did it answer in our words, or did it invent its own?
  2. Did it hand off when it should have, or did it guess?
  3. Is there a question it keeps getting wrong? That is a line missing from the brief.

Each fix is one sentence added to the brief. A month of ten-minute check-ins turns a generic tool into something that sounds like your best employee on their best day — and unlike a person, it keeps every correction.

When it gets something wrong

It will. Treat it the way you would treat a new hire's mistake: fix the brief, not the person — there is no person. If the tool promised a discount you do not offer, the brief never said what to say about price. If it argued with an unhappy customer, the brief never said when to hand off. Blaming the tool is like blaming the new starter for the training you skipped.

That is the honest trade. AI tools do not replace judgment; they multiply whatever judgment you put into the brief. Put in a clear one and the tool works while you sleep. Put in a vague one and it will be vague, politely, all night long.

The one-sentence version: an AI tool is only as good as the brief you give it — write the brief you would leave for a new hire on their first day, then spend ten minutes a week keeping it honest.
