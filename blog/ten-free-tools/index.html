We built ten free tools for local businesses — here is why

No account, no payment, no catch — just ten small tools that each do one small job local businesses run into every week. They are live now on this website, and this post is the honest explanation: what they are, why most of them never touch our computers, and why a business that sells services would give tools away for free.

Nona Digi · October 30, 2026 · 6 min read

Small jobs, all day long

Running a local business means a constant stream of small digital chores that have nothing to do with the actual work. A photo from a phone is too large to upload to your website. A supplier sends a contract, then a revised contract, and somebody has to work out what changed. A happy customer says "I would love to leave you a review" and then cannot find where to do it. A photograph of the shop, posted in a hurry, quietly carries the exact location it was taken.

None of these jobs justifies a software subscription. Each one is five or ten minutes of fiddling — but they land in the middle of a working day, they interrupt the real work, and they never stop coming. So we built a tool for each of them, and put them on one page where anyone can use them.

The ten tools

Five of them are for photographs, which is where most of the daily fiddling happens:

  • Image Compressor — makes a photo's file smaller so it uploads quickly and keeps your pages fast, without turning it into a blurry mess.
  • Image Resizer — gets a picture to the exact size a profile, listing, or social post asks for, instead of whatever size the phone felt like taking it.
  • Merge Two Images — puts a before and an after side by side in one picture, the way customers actually judge a job.
  • Image Format Converter — turns one kind of image file into another when a website or a form insists on a format your phone did not give you.
  • Photo Metadata Remover — strips the hidden details saved inside a photo, including where it was taken, before you post it anywhere public.

Two handle words and paperwork:

  • Compare Two Documents — shows you exactly what changed between two versions of a document, line by line, so a revised quote or contract holds no surprises.
  • Review QR Code Generator — makes a printable code for your counter or tables, so a happy customer can scan it and leave a review while the good feeling is still fresh.

And three are aimed squarely at growth:

  • Frequently Asked Questions Generator — drafts a starter set of questions and answers from your own business details, written only from what you give it, so your website starts answering customers properly.
  • Review Reply Helper — drafts polite replies to reviews, glowing or grumpy, in the tone you choose, ready for you to check and post.
  • After-Hours Missed-Lead Calculator — a plain estimate of what unanswered evening and weekend enquiries may be costing you, using your own numbers.

Your files stay on your device

Here is the part we care about most. Most of these tools run entirely in your web browser. When you shrink a photo or clean its hidden details, the picture never leaves your device — it is not uploaded to us, it is not stored anywhere, and when you close the page it is gone. We could not look at your files if we wanted to, and we think that is how tools like this should work.

Two of them — the frequently asked questions generator and the review reply helper — work differently, and we would rather say so plainly: they use AI on our side to draft the writing, so the text you type in is sent to do that job. Everything else happens on your own machine.

Why give them away?

Three honest reasons. First, useful things get shared. A tool that saves somebody ten minutes gets bookmarked, sent to a friend who runs a shop, and linked from other websites. That is simply how small businesses have always heard about each other — a recommendation travels further than an advertisement.

Second, a few readers will one day need what we actually sell. Our work is helping local businesses get recommended by AI assistants and making sure someone answers when customers come calling. If that day comes for you, we would rather you remembered the people who handed you something useful for free than the ones who only ever sent you a pitch.

Third, building them taught us things. Sitting with these ten small jobs showed us where local businesses really lose time — not in grand strategy, but in photographs, paperwork, and replies that wait until midnight. That understanding makes our paid work better, and it cost us nothing but the building.

The one-sentence version: the tools are free because useful beats clever — they solve real ten-minute problems, they keep your files on your own device, and if we ever earn your business, we would rather it started this way.
