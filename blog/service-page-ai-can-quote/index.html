How to write a service page an AI assistant can quote

When a customer asks an AI assistant who to hire, the assistant doesn't browse your website the way a person does — it lifts sentences. Pages that state plainly what the service is, who it's for, where you work, and how booking works are the ones that get quoted. Vague pages get skipped. Here's how to write the quotable kind.

Nona Digi · October 21, 2026 · 6 min read

The assistant is hunting for sentences it can lift

Ask an AI assistant for a recommendation and it builds the answer out of material it can stand behind: a service named clearly, a town named specifically, a process described step by step. It is, in effect, quoting you to your next customer. That work — being easy to quote — is the heart of answer engine optimization and generative engine optimization, and it happens one page at a time.

Now read your own service page through that lens. If it says you offer "quality service at fair prices" and "solutions tailored to your needs," the assistant has nothing to lift. Those sentences could belong to any business in any town, so they belong to none. The page feels reassuring to a human skimming it, but to an assistant it's empty — and empty pages don't get quoted.

The anatomy of a quotable service page

A quotable page answers five questions in plain sentences, high up, before any decoration:

  • What is this service? Name it the way customers name it. "Deep cleaning for homes" beats "comprehensive residential refresh solutions."
  • Who is it for? Busy families, landlords between tenants, offices after hours — say it. An assistant matching a customer to a business needs to know the fit.
  • Where do you do it? Name the towns and neighborhoods you actually serve. "Serving the greater area" is a shrug; a list of real places is a fact an assistant can use.
  • What does it involve? Walk through what's included, step by step. What happens first, what you bring, what the customer needs to do, how long it usually takes.
  • How does booking work? Book online any time, call during office hours, we confirm by text — whatever is true. The last step should be as clear as the first.

And the question everyone dodges: what does it cost? If you can publish real prices, publish them — a page that says what a service costs is the single most quotable page in most local markets, because so few competitors will do it. If your prices honestly depend on the job, say that in the shape of the truth instead: how pricing works, what it depends on, whether estimates are free, whether there's a minimum. Never invent a figure to look complete. An honest explanation of how you price is quotable; a made-up number is a liability.

Before and after: the same service, two pages

Here's the difference in practice. This is a generic example — a fictional home cleaning company — but the pattern applies to every trade and profession we work with.

Before — vague marketing language

"Welcome to Sunshine Clean, your trusted partner in pristine living spaces! We deliver top-quality cleaning solutions tailored to your unique needs, with a commitment to excellence in everything we do. Our experienced team is passionate about transforming homes across the region. Contact us today for a sparkling tomorrow!"

After — plain, quotable facts

"Sunshine Clean provides recurring house cleaning in Winter Park, Maitland, and Casselberry. A standard visit covers kitchens, bathrooms, bedrooms, and living areas: surfaces wiped, floors vacuumed and mopped, bathrooms sanitized, and trash removed. Most three-bedroom homes take about two hours. We bring all supplies. Cleaning is priced by the home's size and condition, and every estimate is free — book online any time and we'll confirm your slot by text."

Count what the second paragraph gives an assistant: a service, three towns, a list of what's included, a typical duration, what's provided, how pricing works, and exactly how to book. Any one of those sentences can be lifted straight into an answer. The first paragraph gives it nothing but adjectives.

Write it the way you'd say it on the phone

The simplest test we know: if a customer called and asked what you do, you'd answer in short, plain sentences with real nouns — the service, the towns, the steps, the price shape, the next step. Write the page that way. Short sentences. Real place names. The words your customers use, not the words your industry uses. And give each service its own page: a page about one service, in one area, written plainly, is far easier to quote than a single page trying to cover everything you do.

Two more habits help. Keep the facts current — hours, areas, and what's included change, and a page that contradicts your Google Business Profile confuses assistants and customers alike. And state only what you can stand behind: licenses you hold, guarantees you actually honor, areas you genuinely serve. Assistants cross-check claims across the web; a claim that falls apart costs more than it earns.

The one-sentence test: pick any paragraph on your service page and ask — could an assistant lift one sentence from this and answer a customer's question with it? If the answer is no for most of the page, the page is decoration. Rewrite it until the answer is yes.
