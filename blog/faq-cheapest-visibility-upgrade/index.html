Frequently asked questions: the cheapest visibility upgrade on your website

Every week, your customers ask you the same handful of questions — on the phone, by email, across the counter. Those questions, answered plainly on one page of your website, are the highest-value writing you will ever publish. And the raw material is already sitting in your call log and your inbox.

Nona Digi · September 16, 2026 · 5 min read

One page, three audiences

Most website improvements help one audience and ignore the others. A good questions-and-answers page is the rare change that serves three at once:

  • Nervous first-time customers. People hesitate before they call. They worry about cost, about how long things take, about whether they will be talked into something they do not need. A straight answer on your website removes the fear that was stopping the phone from ringing.
  • Search engines. Customers type their questions into search word for word. A page that asks and answers those exact questions, in plain language, is a page a search engine can confidently show.
  • AI assistants. When someone asks an assistant a question about your kind of business, the assistant goes looking for a quotable answer. A short question followed by a clear, honest answer is the easiest thing in the world for it to find, trust, and repeat — this is answer engine optimization and generative engine optimization in their simplest form: be the business whose answers can be quoted.

No other page on your site works that hard for that little money. There is no advertising spend, no new software, no redesign. There is one afternoon of writing.

Start with the questions you already hear

The most common mistake is sitting down with a blank page and trying to imagine what customers might ask. You do not have to imagine anything. Your real questions are already recorded — you have just never gathered them in one place:

  • Your phone calls. For one week, keep a notepad by the phone. Every time a caller asks something you have answered before, write it down in their words, not yours.
  • Your inbox and messages. Search your email and your website enquiries for question marks. The same five or six questions will surface again and again.
  • Your front desk and your crew. The people who talk to customers all day carry the list in their heads. Ask them: "What do people always ask before they book?" Then ask what people ask when they are worried, because worried questions are the ones that decide who gets the job.
  • Your reviews. Happy reviews often name the thing a customer was unsure about first. Unhappy ones often name the question nobody answered in time.

By the end of the week you will have more questions than you need. Pick the ten or twelve that come up most, and especially the ones asked before a customer commits — price, timing, guarantees, what happens on the day, what happens if something goes wrong.

Write answers the way you talk

Answer each question the way you would answer it across the counter: short, direct, and in your own words. Two or four sentences is usually plenty. Say the number if there is a number. Say the condition if there is a condition. "Most single visits start at this price; if we find something more, we tell you before we begin" builds more trust than a paragraph of careful vagueness — and it is exactly the kind of sentence an AI assistant can quote without mangling it.

One rule matters more than all the others: never publish an answer that is not actually true of your business. Do not promise same-day service you cannot always deliver, a guarantee you have not thought through, or opening hours you keep only in a good week. An answer on your website is a promise. If a policy does not exist yet, the honest move is to decide the policy first and then write it down — many owners find this page quietly forces the useful decisions they had been postponing.

A quick test for every answer: read it out loud. If you would not say it to a customer's face, it does not belong on the page.

Keep it alive, a little at a time

A questions page is not finished when it is published; it is started. Every genuinely new question a customer asks is a candidate for the page. Prices change, services change, a new team member starts answering the phone differently — the page should follow. Put a recurring note in your calendar once a quarter: read the page, fix anything that has drifted, add the two or three new questions the season brought. That small habit is what keeps the page true, and a true page is the whole point — customers, search engines, and AI assistants all reward the business whose answers match reality.

There is a second payoff, closer to home: your team starts answering consistently. When the same question gets the same answer from everyone — because it is written down — customers feel they are dealing with one business that knows itself. That feeling is worth more than the traffic.

The afternoon version

If this all sounds sensible but large, shrink it. Gather ten questions this week. Write ten honest answers next week, in your own words, the way you talk. Put them on one page with a clear heading. That is the upgrade. Everything after that is refinement — and you will still be ahead of every competitor whose website says "quality service at fair prices" and answers nothing at all.
