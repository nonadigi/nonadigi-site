Reviews Are the Résumé AI Assistants Read Before Recommending You

Ask an AI assistant for a good plumber, dentist, or salon near you, and it answers with a name — usually one name, offered with confidence. Before it picked that name, it did what a careful customer does: it read the reviews. Think of your reviews as the résumé an assistant reads on your behalf. The question is whether yours looks current, specific, and alive.

Nona Digi · September 9, 2026 · 6 min read

What the assistant is looking for

An assistant cannot visit your shop or shake your hand. To recommend you, it needs evidence it can read — and reviews are the richest evidence most local businesses have. This is the heart of answer engine optimization and generative engine optimization: assistants repeat what the public record supports. Two qualities in that record matter more than owners expect:

  • Recency. A steady trickle of reviews from this season says the business is open, busy, and consistent. A wall of praise from three years ago, followed by silence, reads like a business that may have changed hands — or closed.
  • Specific detail. "They fixed our water heater the same day and the technician explained everything" gives an assistant something true and concrete to work with. "Great service" gives it almost nothing.

Raw count matters less than owners fear. A competitor with more reviews overall but nothing recent and nothing specific is a weaker résumé than yours with fewer, fresher, richer ones. We would rather help a business build the second kind.

Ask at the happy moment

Nearly every business has happy customers who never leave a review. The usual reason is not reluctance — it is timing. The customer meant to, the moment passed, and life moved on. So the craft is in the asking, and the asking has a right moment: right when the good feeling is freshest. The repair works and the house is cool again. The crown fits perfectly. The haircut gets its first compliment.

At that moment, a short, human ask works: "Reviews are how people find us — would you mind sharing how today went?" Then stop. No pressure, no script read at someone, no asking twice. A customer who is asked warmly, once, at the right moment usually says yes — and writes a better review than any form letter could produce, because the details are still in front of them.

Make it one tap

Goodwill evaporates with every extra step. If leaving a review means searching for the business, scrolling, and signing in twice, most people quietly give up — even the ones who promised. Remove the friction:

  • One short link that opens your review page directly, used in texts, emails, and receipts.
  • A printed code at the counter — a small card or sign the customer scans with a phone camera and lands straight on the review page. Tape it where people wait: the register, the front desk, the checkout counter.
  • The same link everywhere, so the ask and the path never change.

If you want to try the card, our free review code generator makes one in about a minute. However you do it, test the path yourself on your own phone first. If it takes you more than a few seconds, it will stop your customers too.

Reply to every review — it is part of the résumé

Here is the part most businesses skip, and it may be the most human signal on the page. When the owner replies — briefly, warmly, by name where possible — the profile stops looking like a listing and starts looking like a relationship. Customers reading replies think: these people pay attention. Assistants reading the same page reach a similar conclusion from the evidence available to them.

Good replies are a craft, and a learnable one:

  • Thank the reviewer for something specific they mentioned, not a pasted "thanks for your feedback."
  • Keep it short. Three or four sentences is plenty.
  • Answer hard reviews calmly. Acknowledge the experience, state what you did or will do, and invite the conversation offline. Never argue. A graceful reply to a rough review often persuades readers more than the stars do.
  • Reply like a habit, not a campaign — a few minutes, a couple of times a week, forever.

The one rule with no exceptions

Never buy reviews. Never invent them. Never have family, staff, or friends pose as customers, and never offer a discount or a gift in exchange for one. This is not only about honesty, though honesty would be reason enough. Fake reviews are against the platforms' rules, they put the whole profile at risk, and they are usually obvious — a sudden burst of vague five-star praise reads as staged to customers and assistants alike. One honest, specific, three-sentence review from a real customer is worth more than a stack of purchased ones, because it is the only kind anyone believes.

The habit, in one line: ask once at the happy moment, make it one tap, reply to everything, and let real customers write your résumé one honest paragraph at a time.
