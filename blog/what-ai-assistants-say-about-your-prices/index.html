What an AI Assistant Says When a Customer Asks What You Charge

Your next customer already knows what the job is. What they do not know is what it costs, so they ask. Not you, first. They ask an AI assistant: how much does a drain clearing cost, what does a dentist charge for a cleaning, what is the price of a furnace tune-up near me. The assistant answers with the businesses that put a number, or at least a range, where it can find one. If your website never mentions money, you are not in that answer.

Nona Digi · November 6, 2026 · 5 min read

The price question comes before the phone call

Think about the last time you hired someone for work on your own home. Before you called anyone, you wanted a rough idea of the cost. Not a binding quote, just a sense of the ballpark. This is the quiet part of AI visibility, which is also called answer engine optimization and generative engine optimization: being recommended for the job, with a price the customer can picture.

Your customers do the same, and more of them now ask an AI assistant. They type a question the way they would say it to a neighbour: what does a water heater replacement cost, how much is a teeth cleaning without insurance, what do Christmas light installers charge for a two-storey house. The assistant reads what local businesses have published and gives an answer with names attached. The businesses that get named are the ones that gave it something to quote.

What the assistant does when it finds nothing

When an assistant cannot find any price information for your business, it does not say you are too expensive. It does not say anything about you at all. It simply reaches for the next business that did publish something, and quotes that number instead. You lose the job before anyone has compared your work, your reviews, or your reputation, because the comparison never happened. The customer got an answer, it had a number in it, and your name was not in the sentence.

Most owners who keep prices off their website do it to avoid scaring people away, or because every job is different and a single number would be misleading. Both worries are reasonable. But the alternative is not a customer who calls you to find out the price. The alternative, more and more, is a customer who never calls at all, because the assistant handed them a competitor with a number and they moved on.

“Call for a quote” is an answer too, and it costs you

If your website says “call for pricing” on every service, you have given the assistant an answer, just not a useful one. It cannot repeat “call for a quote” as a price, so it treats your business the same way it treats one with no price information at all: it moves on to one it can quote.

There is a second cost, and it falls on the customer. Asking for a price is the lowest-effort question a customer can ask. Making them call, wait, and explain their situation just to learn the ballpark is the highest-effort way to answer it. Many will not bother, not because they are lazy, but because the business down the road already answered the question on its website.

A range is enough

You do not need to publish a fixed price list, and for most trades you should not. Every job is different, and a flat number that turns out wrong helps nobody. What an assistant needs is a range it can repeat, and what a customer needs is a range they can plan around. Those are the same thing.

Write it the way you would say it to a customer standing in front of you. A drain clearing usually runs between one amount and another, depending on how bad the blockage is. A teeth cleaning has a price for patients with insurance and a price for patients without it. Add the one sentence that matters most: what changes the price. Size of the house, age of the system, whether parts are included. That sentence turns a bare number into an honest answer, and it is exactly the kind of detail an assistant can quote word for word.

Nobody expects the figure on your website to be the final invoice. They expect it to be close enough to decide whether to call, and honest enough that the call does not start with a surprise. A range that is roughly right beats a silence that says nothing.

Put the numbers where an assistant can quote them

A price buried in a PDF, hidden behind a form, or mentioned only in a social media post from two years ago is almost as invisible as none at all. Assistants quote what they can read plainly on a page, in ordinary sentences, next to the service it belongs to.

The best place is the service page itself, in the body of the page, not in a footnote. A short paragraph near the top, in plain language: what the service costs, what is included, and what would push it higher. Your frequently asked questions page is the second-best place, because customers ask about price there first, and assistants read those pages closely.

Keep the wording the same everywhere. If your website says one range, your business profile says another, and a directory listing says a third, the assistant has three conflicting answers and no reason to trust any of them. One number, in one place, repeated consistently, is more quotable than three numbers that disagree.

The one-hour version of this

You do not need a website rebuild to fix this. Pick your three most-asked-for jobs, and for each one, write three sentences: what it usually costs, what is included, and what changes the price. Put those sentences on the matching service page, and repeat the same wording on your frequently asked questions page. Then check your business profile and your main directory listings to make sure nothing there contradicts them. That is about an hour of work, and it is the kind of work that keeps paying: every time a customer asks an assistant what you charge, there is now an answer with your name in it.

And if the range you publish brings in a caller whose job turns out bigger than the range, that is not a failure of the page. That is the page doing its job. The caller came to you already knowing the ballpark, and the conversation starts from trust instead of a cold quote request.

The one-sentence version: customers ask an AI assistant what you charge before they call you, and the assistant quotes the businesses that published a range it can repeat, so a rough number on your service page is worth more than a perfect price list you never publish.
