Your Google Business Profile Is Your New Homepage: A Plain-English Tune-Up

When a customer searches for what you do, the page they actually read is often not your website. It is your Google Business Profile — the box with your name, hours, photos, and reviews. Many people call straight from it without ever visiting your homepage. And when someone asks an AI assistant instead, the assistant leans on these same details. Here is how to tune the profile up, field by field.

Nona Digi · September 2, 2026 · 6 min read

The page your customers read first

Picture a homeowner with a leaking water heater, or a couple looking for a dentist near their new house. They search on their phone, and your profile answers the questions that decide the call: Are they open? How far away? What do the reviews say? Can I see the place? If the answers are clear and current, the phone rings. If a field is blank, stale, or confusing, they move to the next name — usually without ever learning what your website says.

This is also why the profile matters for answer engine optimization and generative engine optimization. AI assistants build their recommendations from sources they can read and verify, and a complete, consistent business profile is one of the clearest sources there is. We cannot promise any particular ranking or recommendation — nobody honestly can. What we can say is simpler: these are the details customers and assistants read, so they should be right.

Start with the name and categories

Your business name on the profile should be your real business name — the one on your sign and your paperwork — not a string of keywords. Stuffing extra words into the name can get a profile flagged, and it looks desperate to anyone who reads it. Let categories do the describing instead.

Pick the primary category that matches the main thing customers hire you for, then add every secondary category that honestly applies. Categories are how Google and assistants sort you into the right conversations. A plumber listed only as a general contractor is invisible in the conversations that matter most. Spend ten minutes here; it is the highest-value field on the page.

List services in the words customers use

The services section is where most profiles quietly underperform. Owners write in trade language — the terms used inside the business — while customers search in everyday language. The customer does not ask for "HVAC system diagnostics." She says "my air conditioner is blowing warm air." List your services the second way:

  • One service per line, named the way a customer would describe the job.
  • Cover the whole menu — the routine work, the emergency work, and the jobs you wish you got more of.
  • Add a short description where the profile allows it, and honest pricing where you are comfortable sharing it. Specifics are what assistants can repeat.

Read your list out loud. If it sounds like a customer describing their problem, it is written correctly.

Keep hours honest — including the odd days

Wrong hours are the fastest way to lose a customer you had already won. Someone drives across town because your profile said open, finds a locked door, and leaves a review about that instead of about your work. Set your regular hours carefully, then do the step most businesses skip: set special hours for holidays and seasonal changes before they arrive.

If your hours differ by day, or you close for lunch, or Saturdays are appointment-only, say exactly that. Assistants quote these fields word for word when someone asks "are they open now?" An honest "closed today" costs you nothing. A wrong "open" costs you trust.

Show the real place and the real people

Photos are proof. Customers use them to decide whether a business feels real, cared for, and safe to call — and a profile with no photos reads like a business that might not be there anymore. You do not need a professional shoot. You need honest pictures, refreshed a few times a year:

  • The storefront or sign, shot the way a customer arriving would see it.
  • The inside: the front desk, the waiting area, the bays, the chairs — clean and in daylight.
  • The team, smiling, in work clothes. Faces build more trust than logos.
  • The work itself: before-and-after pairs, finished jobs, the truck or van customers should expect at the door.

Answer the questions section before someone else does

Most profiles have a questions-and-answers area that anyone can post in — including strangers answering on your behalf, sometimes wrongly. Owners can post their own common questions and answer them: where to park, whether walk-ins are welcome, what to bring to a first appointment, how estimates work. Filling this in takes half an hour once, and it means the answers customers read are yours, in your words, and correct. Check it now and then, the same way you would check a notice board with your name on it.

Build the review habit, not the review campaign

Finally, the field everyone notices first: reviews. The goal is not a one-time push; it is a steady habit — ask at the happy moment, make leaving a review one simple tap, and reply to every review you receive. A profile with fresh reviews and thoughtful replies reads as a business that is alive and paying attention, to customers and to assistants alike. We wrote a whole post on this — why AI assistants recommend businesses with recent reviews — because it matters that much.

The one-sitting version: set aside one hour. Fix the name and categories, list services in customer words, confirm hours and set holiday hours, add a dozen honest photos, seed the questions section, and ask your last three happy customers for a review. That single hour covers everything in this post.
